Bragg’s Law as an Evidence Graph

Evidence graph of Scilib.Bragg, written in Lean 4

October 21, 2026

AbstractIf the atoms in a crystal sit in neat, evenly spaced planes, X‑rays bounce off those planes and add up only at certain angles. That is Bragg’s law, and it is why a crystal in an X‑ray beam produces sharp spikes instead of a smooth blur. This page draws the argument as a graph: which ideas lead to the prediction, which experiment tests it, and what the evidence says in two different worlds, one where the target is a crystal and one where it has no atomic order. Pick a world below, and hover or tap any item for its full statement.

1 The evidence graph

Figure 1: Read the graph from left to right. Each box on the left is an idea, and each hollow square says “this idea leads to that prediction”. The filled square says “this prediction was tested by that experiment”. The small coloured dots on a box show which worlds include it: blue for the crystal world, teal for the amorphous world. Faded boxes are not part of the world you selected, and a dashed outline means the box is not connected to any arrow. Under the experiment you can read the verdict for each world in words.

Table 1: The verdict for the one experiment in the graph. “Supported” means the world’s ideas lead to the prediction that the experiment tested. “Silent” means they do not, so the experiment says nothing about that world. The selected world is shaded.
